Saturn

Saturn's rings: young, thin and temporary?

Spanning nearly 280,000 kilometres — wide enough to fit almost two Earths side by side — yet mostly only tens to hundreds of metres thick, Saturn's rings are one of the Solar System's most extraordinary structures. They are also, scientists increasingly suspect, far younger than Saturn itself and slowly disappearing.

What the rings are made of

Saturn's main rings are composed overwhelmingly of water ice — estimates suggest roughly 90–95% by mass — along with small amounts of rock and dust. The ice comes in chunks ranging from tiny grains to boulders several metres across. That ice is strikingly pure, which is one of the clues to the rings' age: over long timescales, dust and micrometeorite bombardment gradually darken ring material, making it redder and dirtier. Saturn's rings are still quite bright and white, which suggests they have not been accumulating contamination for very long.

How thin are they really?

The contrast between the rings' breadth and their thinness is one of astronomy's most striking facts. While the main rings spread out to about 280,000 km from Saturn's centre, their vertical thickness across most of that span is estimated at between about 10 and a few hundred metres. If you scaled the rings to the size of a piece of paper, the paper would be hundreds of times thicker than the rings are, proportionally.

This extreme flatness arises because ring particles follow nearly the same orbital plane and any particle that strays vertically is quickly pulled back by gravity — either Saturn's or that of its moons, which act as "shepherds" that confine certain ring edges.

Are the rings young?

The age of Saturn's rings is a genuine scientific debate. For a long time, many astronomers assumed the rings were ancient — roughly as old as Saturn itself, around 4.5 billion years. The argument was that any ring system forming early would naturally survive.

Cassini mission data, however, pointed in a different direction. Measurements of the rings' mass (much lower than expected for a very old, accumulated system) and their brightness (suggesting they have not darkened over billions of years) led a number of researchers to propose the rings formed only about 100 to 400 million years ago. On that timescale, the dinosaurs were already walking on Earth when Saturn's rings took shape. The proposed mechanism is the tidal disruption of a large icy moon or a comet that came too close.

This hypothesis is not universally accepted. Some researchers argue that the rings could be old and have been replenished or that mass estimates carry large uncertainties. The debate is active.

Ring rain and the rings' fate

Cassini also detected what researchers call "ring rain" — charged ice particles being dragged out of the rings along magnetic field lines and falling into Saturn's upper atmosphere. The rate at which this happens led some scientists to estimate that the rings could erode away entirely within tens to hundreds of millions of years, a relatively short time by astronomical standards.

If the young-ring hypothesis is correct, we live at a fortunate moment: Saturn's rings may be only a temporary feature of the Solar System, and we happen to exist while they are still there to see.
