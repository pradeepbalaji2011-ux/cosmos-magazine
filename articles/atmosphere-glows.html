Earth from orbit: why our atmosphere glows

Seen from the International Space Station, Earth's atmosphere is not simply a transparent veil around the planet. It is alive with colour — a thin, luminous shell that shimmers green, orange and electric blue against the darkness of space, produced by chemistry and physics that never stop.

The airglow layer

On most clear nights, every square centimetre of sky above you is faintly glowing. This is airglow, a permanent, low-level luminescence produced by chemical reactions in the upper atmosphere. Unlike aurora, which requires energetic charged particles from the Sun, airglow happens continuously and globally — it is a product of the atmosphere going about its ordinary business.

The main source of airglow sits at altitudes of roughly 85 to 95 kilometres, near the boundary between the mesosphere and thermosphere. During the day, solar ultraviolet radiation breaks apart molecules of oxygen and nitrogen. After sunset, some of those atoms and ions recombine and shed their excess energy as light. The most prominent emission is a green glow produced by excited oxygen atoms at around 557.7 nanometres — visible to cameras on the ISS as a delicate arc along the horizon.

From the ground, airglow is far too faint to see with the naked eye against a typical sky, but it is bright enough to be a limiting factor for ground-based telescopes. Wide-field astronomical imaging at dark sites often reveals rippling wave-like patterns in the airglow, caused by atmospheric gravity waves — oscillations in the air created by weather systems, mountains, and other disturbances far below.

Why the horizon glows orange and green

Looking toward Earth's limb from orbit, astronauts see layers stacked like a painting. Closest to the surface, the troposphere scatters sunlight into warm orange and red tones at twilight. Above that, the stratosphere and mesosphere appear darker. Then, at the airglow layer, a distinct band of green or greenish-yellow light traces the horizon, fading into the black of space above it.

Sodium atoms swept into the atmosphere by meteors also contribute a yellow-orange emission layer centred around 90 kilometres altitude. This sodium layer is thin — only a few kilometres deep — but intense enough that astronomers use it to calibrate laser guide stars in adaptive optics systems, which correct for atmospheric turbulence in real time.

Noctilucent clouds and aurora

Two other atmospheric light shows are visible from orbit and occasionally from the ground. Noctilucent clouds — also called polar mesospheric clouds — form at altitudes of about 80 to 85 kilometres, where temperatures can drop low enough for water vapour to freeze onto meteoric dust. They appear as silver-blue, rippling streaks visible to observers at high latitudes in summer twilight, when the sun is just below the horizon but still illuminates these very high clouds.

Aurora occur at much higher altitudes, typically between 100 and 300 kilometres, when energetic electrons and protons from the Sun are funnelled by Earth's magnetic field into the polar regions. They collide with oxygen and nitrogen atoms, producing curtains of green, red and purple light. From the ISS, orbiting at about 400 kilometres, crew members can look down on aurora spreading across entire countries, a perspective utterly unlike the view from below.

What astronauts see

Astronauts have consistently described Earth's glowing atmosphere as one of the most striking aspects of life in orbit. Photographs taken from the ISS since the early 2000s have provided invaluable scientific data as well as some of the most iconic images of Earth from space. The airglow appears consistently in long-exposure ISS photography, confirming that it is a permanent feature — a quiet reminder that Earth's atmosphere is not inert but is constantly interacting with sunlight, space weather, and its own internal dynamics.

The thinness of the glow relative to the planet is also sobering. The entire layer responsible for sheltering life on Earth's surface from harmful radiation — and for producing all the luminous beauty visible from orbit — extends only a few hundred kilometres above the surface. In photographs, it looks like the thinnest coat of paint on a large ball.
