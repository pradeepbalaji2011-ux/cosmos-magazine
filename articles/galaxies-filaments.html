How galaxies grow filaments of hot gas

The largest structures in the universe are not galaxies or even clusters of galaxies. They are vast threads of hot gas, stretching tens of millions of light-years, that link galaxy clusters together in a pattern astronomers call the cosmic web.

What are cosmic filaments?

Look at a computer simulation of the universe at the largest scales and you will see something that resembles a soap bubble foam or a neural network. Dense knots — galaxy clusters containing hundreds or thousands of galaxies — sit at the intersections of thin, thread-like structures that weave between vast empty voids. These threads are the cosmic filaments.

The filaments are not empty. They carry gas that has been heated to temperatures of roughly 100,000 to 10 million kelvin as it falls along gravitational gradients built up over billions of years. This gas is sometimes called the warm-hot intergalactic medium, or WHIM, and it represents a large reservoir of ordinary matter that is genuinely difficult to detect. Because it is so hot, it emits mostly soft X-rays and ultraviolet light — wavelengths easily absorbed by our galaxy's own gas and dust before reaching our telescopes.

Simulations based on the standard cosmological model have predicted for decades that filaments must exist and that they should contain a substantial fraction of all ordinary (baryonic) matter in the universe. Pinning down that fraction observationally remains a challenge, but estimates suggest it could be 40 to 50 percent — though this figure is actively debated.

How gas reaches galaxies

Filaments are more than passive features. They act as highways, funnelling gas toward galaxy clusters and, to some extent, toward individual galaxies. Early in the universe's history, before gravity had fully assembled the web, gas flowed more freely. As dark matter halos grew and merged, ordinary matter followed, streaming along filaments into the proto-clusters forming at their intersections.

For a galaxy sitting in or near a filament, this inflow can supply fresh hydrogen — the fuel for star formation. Some researchers think that cold streams of gas threading through hot filament gas may deliver material deep into galaxies without the gas being shock-heated and stalled. This "cold accretion" mode is thought to be important for building galaxies, especially in the early universe, though the observational picture is still being assembled.

Detecting the invisible

Because filament gas is so diffuse and spread across enormous distances, detecting it directly is one of the harder problems in observational cosmology. Several strategies are being used. X-ray telescopes search for thermal emission from the hottest filament gas. Stacking analyses — averaging the X-ray signal from the spaces between many pairs of known galaxy clusters — have turned up statistical evidence for filament gas. The eROSITA all-sky X-ray survey, launched in 2019, has begun imaging individual filaments between relatively nearby cluster pairs.

A complementary technique uses the Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect: hot gas distorts the spectrum of the cosmic microwave background in a characteristic way, and this signal can be searched for along lines connecting clusters in maps from telescopes like Planck. These approaches have provided some of the clearest evidence yet that filaments are real physical structures containing hot gas, not just features of simulations.

What filaments tell us

Beyond mapping where matter lives, filaments carry information about the history of the universe. The distribution, temperature and density of filament gas encode the combined effects of dark matter gravity, galaxy feedback (energy injected by supernovae and black holes), and cosmic expansion. Comparing observed filaments with simulations provides a way to test cosmological models and to probe the properties of dark matter and dark energy.

There is also a missing-baryons problem that filaments may help resolve: accounting for all the ordinary matter predicted by the Big Bang nucleosynthesis models has been difficult because not all of it shows up in stars and galaxies. Filaments may be where much of that missing matter has been hiding, heated to temperatures that made it nearly invisible until sufficiently sensitive X-ray and microwave telescopes could find it.

Key Facts

Filament temperature
100,000–10 million K (warm-hot intergalactic medium)
Typical filament length
Tens to hundreds of millions of light-years
Gas content (estimated)
~40–50% of all ordinary matter (debated)
Key recent detection
eROSITA X-ray survey, 2020s; Planck SZ stacking
