Stellar life cycle

Nebulae: nurseries and graveyards of stars

A nebula is a cloud of gas and dust in space. The word is Latin for "mist" or "cloud", and for centuries it described anything fuzzy in a telescope. Today astronomers recognise several distinct types, united only by their diffuse appearance and their central role in the life and death of stars.

What is a nebula?

In modern usage, a nebula is a cloud of interstellar gas — mostly hydrogen and helium — mixed with dust grains. Nebulae vary enormously in size, density, temperature, and origin. Some are sites of active star formation; others are the remnants of stars that have already died. Several types may coexist in a single region of sky.

Most nebulae are far too faint to see with the naked eye. A handful are close enough and large enough to be visible: the Orion Nebula (M42) is visible as the fuzzy "star" at the centre of Orion's sword. It is one of the most studied astronomical objects in the sky.

Star-forming regions: emission nebulae

Emission nebulae glow because energetic ultraviolet radiation from hot young stars ionises the surrounding hydrogen gas. When the ionised hydrogen recaptures electrons, it releases photons at specific wavelengths — including the characteristic red of hydrogen-alpha emission. This is the colour seen in so many astronomical photographs of star-forming regions, though our eyes, less sensitive to deep red, may perceive these clouds as pink or even green in colour photographs taken with different filter combinations.

The Orion Nebula, about 1,350 light-years away and roughly 24 light-years across, is an emission nebula lit by the Trapezium, a cluster of hot young stars at its core. Hubble images show hundreds of young stars surrounded by protoplanetary discs — the raw material of planetary systems still in formation.

The Eagle Nebula (M16), the Carina Nebula, and the vast Orion Molecular Cloud complex are other well-known star-forming emission nebulae. Inside these regions, dense clumps of cold gas collapse under gravity. As a clump shrinks, its centre heats up until nuclear fusion ignites — a star is born.

Planetary nebulae: how Sun-like stars die

When a star of about one to eight times the Sun's mass exhausts its nuclear fuel, it swells into a red giant and then sheds its outer layers in a relatively gentle process. The expelled gas forms an expanding shell around the exposed stellar core, which is now a hot, dense white dwarf. Ultraviolet radiation from the white dwarf ionises the shell, making it glow — a planetary nebula.

The name "planetary nebula" is historical and misleading: it has nothing to do with planets. William Herschel coined it in the eighteenth century because these objects resembled the disc of a distant planet through a small telescope. The Ring Nebula (M57) in Lyra, about 2,300 light-years away, is a classic example — a glowing doughnut of expelled gas seen nearly face-on. The Helix Nebula (NGC 7293) in Aquarius, about 650 light-years away, is one of the nearest planetary nebulae to Earth.

The Sun will likely form a planetary nebula in about five billion years.

Supernova remnants: death of massive stars

Stars much more massive than the Sun — roughly eight or more solar masses — end their lives violently in supernova explosions. The blast drives an expanding shell of gas outward at thousands of kilometres per second. This shell, heated by the shock wave and by radioactive decay of elements synthesised in the explosion, glows as a supernova remnant.

The Crab Nebula (M1) in Taurus is the remnant of a supernova seen by Chinese, Japanese, and other observers in 1054 CE. It is about 6,500 light-years away and still expanding. At its centre spins a pulsar — a neutron star rotating about 30 times per second — powering the entire cloud's glow with its fast-spinning magnetic field.

Supernova remnants are also factories for heavy elements. The carbon, oxygen, iron, and other elements in your body were forged in stars and dispersed by explosions like these. Nebulae — both the nurseries and the graveyards — are how the material of the universe gets recycled.
