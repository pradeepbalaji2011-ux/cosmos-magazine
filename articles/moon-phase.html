Observing guide

Check the Moon phase before planning dark-sky sessions

A full Moon is roughly 14,000 times brighter than a typical first-magnitude star. Observe on a full-Moon night and the sky background glows bright enough to swamp galaxies, nebulae and faint meteor streams. Scheduling around the lunar cycle costs nothing and gains you a dramatically better sky.

Why the Moon matters

Faint deep-sky objects — galaxies, globular clusters, planetary nebulae — are visible because they are slightly brighter than the surrounding sky background. A bright Moon raises that background, reducing the contrast until faint objects simply disappear. This effect is real even at a dark-sky site: the full Moon turns a dark rural sky into one comparable to a suburban location.

Planets, double stars, and the Moon itself are excellent targets in any phase. For everything else, phase matters enormously.

The lunar cycle

The Moon takes about 29.53 days to complete a full cycle of phases — from new Moon to full Moon and back. This is the synodic period, measured relative to the Sun. (The Moon's orbital period around Earth is slightly shorter, about 27.3 days, but because Earth is also moving around the Sun, the Moon needs a few extra days to "catch up" to the same phase.)

The phases, in order: New Moon → Waxing Crescent → First Quarter → Waxing Gibbous → Full Moon → Waning Gibbous → Last Quarter → Waning Crescent → New Moon again.

At new Moon, the Moon rises and sets roughly with the Sun and is absent from the night sky entirely — perfect for faint-object observing. At full Moon, it is in the sky all night. The five to six days around new Moon (roughly from last quarter to first quarter) are generally the best window for deep-sky work.

Best times to observe

The ideal window for faint objects is the five to seven nights centred on new Moon. At this time, the Moon is either below the horizon during prime observing hours or so thinly crescent that its contribution to sky brightness is negligible. Even a half-Moon (first or last quarter) provides enough stray light to make the sky noticeably brighter than a truly moonless night.

If you must observe during a gibbous or full Moon, concentrate on targets that tolerate bright skies: the Moon itself (through a telescope, it reveals craters and mountains in magnificent detail), planets, bright double stars, and open clusters. Save your deep-sky ambitions for a moonless night.

Using the Sky This Week feature

The Observing section of this magazine calculates the current Moon phase and the dates of the next new and full Moon from your device's clock. No external data is needed. Plan your sessions around the next new Moon — circle it on your calendar and check your local weather forecast.

Many free apps and websites also provide monthly lunar calendars. USNO's online data service gives precise times for new and full Moons years in advance. Remember that the Moon rises roughly 50 minutes later each night, so even a growing crescent that sets early in the evening progressively encroaches on your dark hours as the month progresses.
