The Milky Way

Walking through the spiral arms

On a clear, dark night, a faint band of light arches from horizon to horizon — the combined glow of hundreds of billions of stars so distant they blur together. That band is our own galaxy, seen edge-on from inside. Understanding the Milky Way's structure is one of the great challenges of modern astronomy, made harder by the fact that we cannot step outside to see it.

Our place in the galaxy

The Sun sits roughly 26,000 light-years from the galactic centre — a figure pinned down to better than five percent uncertainty by the GRAVITY Collaboration in 2019, using stellar orbits around the central black hole Sagittarius A*. The Sun is neither at the centre nor at the edge: it lies about halfway out, in a minor feature called the Orion Arm or Orion Spur.

The galaxy's disc extends to perhaps 50,000 light-years from the centre in the direction we can see (some recent studies suggest the outer disc may stretch further, perhaps to 80,000 light-years or beyond, though the boundary is not sharp and estimates vary). A central bar of stars, oriented at roughly 27° to our line of sight to the centre, dominates the inner few thousand light-years.

The four main spiral arms

The Milky Way is a barred spiral galaxy. Beyond the central bar, the disc is structured into spiral arms — regions of higher stellar density, more active star formation, and concentrations of gas and dust. Astronomers debate the exact number and layout of the arms; the most widely used model includes four major arms:

  • Perseus Arm — one of the two most prominent arms, lying further from the centre than the Sun
  • Norma–Outer Arm — on the far side of the galaxy from the Sun
  • Sagittarius–Carina Arm — the arm nearest to the Sun on the inner side, home to many bright nebulae including the Carina Nebula
  • Scutum–Centaurus Arm — anchored to one end of the central bar, rich in star-forming regions

These arm names come from the constellations in which dense arm features appear when viewed from Earth. The arms are not rigid structures but rather density waves — regions where gas piles up and star formation is triggered as the galaxy rotates.

The Orion Arm: our local spur

The Sun lives in the Orion Arm, a shorter, less pronounced feature that lies between the Perseus and Sagittarius arms. It is sometimes called a spur because it branches off from one of the major arms rather than being a major arm in its own right, though its exact classification is not fully settled.

The Orion Arm is roughly 10,000 light-years long and a few thousand light-years wide. It contains many of the bright stars and nebulae we can see from Earth, including Orion's belt and sword, the Pleiades, and nearby stellar nurseries. The closest star to the Sun, Proxima Centauri, is also in this local region, just 4.2 light-years away.

What we see and what we cannot

Because we are embedded in the disc, our view of the Milky Way's large-scale structure is obscured by the very material that makes up the galaxy. Dust in the disc absorbs and scatters visible light, hiding regions more than a few thousand light-years away in most directions. Infrared and radio telescopes can penetrate this dust — it was radio observations of hydrogen gas that first traced the spiral arm structure in the mid-twentieth century.

The European Space Agency's Gaia spacecraft, launched in 2013, has measured the precise positions and motions of well over a billion stars, building the most detailed three-dimensional map of our galaxy yet. Each new data release refines our picture of the Milky Way's structure, history, and future. Gaia has found streams of stars disrupted from swallowed galaxies, mapped the warp of the outer disc, and helped pin down the Sun's precise location.

The Milky Way takes about 225–250 million years to complete one full rotation at the Sun's distance — sometimes called a galactic year. In that time, the Sun has orbited roughly 20 times since the Solar System formed.
