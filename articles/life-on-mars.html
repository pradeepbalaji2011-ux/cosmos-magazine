Mars

The long search for life on Mars

Mars is the most studied planet after Earth. Ancient riverbeds, lake sediments, and minerals that only form in liquid water paint a picture of a world that was once far warmer and wetter. Whether anything ever lived there — and whether anything survives today — remains one of the greatest open questions in science.

A world that once looked promising

In the nineteenth century, astronomers including Giovanni Schiaparelli and Percival Lowell described a network of features they called "canali" — channels — on Mars's surface. Lowell interpreted these as artificial irrigation canals, evidence of a civilisation managing a drying world. Better telescopes and, eventually, spacecraft revealed no such canals: the features were optical illusions. But the impulse that drove those observations — the hope of finding life — was not wrong to target Mars.

Mars today is cold, dry, and thin-aired, with a surface pressure less than one percent of Earth's at sea level. Ultraviolet radiation reaches the surface unimpeded because Mars has no ozone layer and only a trace atmosphere. Yet the planet preserves ancient geology spanning more than four billion years. Some of that geology speaks of a very different early Mars.

Viking: the first direct test

In 1976, NASA's Viking 1 and Viking 2 landers became the first spacecraft to conduct biology experiments on another world. Each carried four biology instruments designed to detect microbial metabolism in the Martian soil. The results were ambiguous and remain debated. One experiment — the labelled-release experiment — initially produced a positive signal, but most scientists attributed it to reactive oxidising chemicals in the Martian soil rather than biology. No organic compounds were detected, and the scientific consensus settled on a non-biological explanation, though a minority of researchers have continued to argue otherwise.

Modern rovers and what they have found

The Curiosity rover, which landed in Gale Crater in 2012, confirmed that the site was once a lake with water chemically compatible with life as we know it. It detected complex organic molecules preserved in sedimentary rocks — not proof of life, but the kind of chemistry that life uses and that could survive over geological timescales. Curiosity also detected seasonal fluctuations in atmospheric methane, though the source (geological or biological) has not been determined.

The Perseverance rover landed in Jezero Crater in February 2021. Jezero is a former lake delta — precisely the kind of environment where sedimentary rocks might trap and preserve signs of ancient life. Perseverance has been drilling rock cores and sealing them for eventual return to Earth, where they can be analysed with laboratory instruments far more powerful than any that can be landed on Mars.

Where the search stands today

No life has been detected on Mars. The search is not over. The Mars Sample Return mission, a joint project of NASA and ESA, aims to bring Perseverance's rock cores back to Earth for analysis — the most ambitious planetary science undertaking yet planned. Whether ancient Mars hosted microbial life (if it did, it would likely have been underground or near hydrothermal vents), and whether any life might persist today in subsurface brines or other refuges, are questions that can only be answered by more data.

All life-detection claims about Mars remain scientifically contested. The scientific consensus, as of the time of writing, is that no confirmed evidence of Martian life — past or present — exists.
