The Sun

Solar cycles explained, from spots to storms

The Sun is not a steady lamp. Its activity — measured in sunspots, flares and the gusts of charged particles it throws into space — waxes and wanes on a roughly 11-year cycle that has shaped Earth's upper atmosphere, power grids and even historical records of naked-eye spots on the Sun's face.

What is the solar cycle?

The solar cycle is a periodic rise and fall in the Sun's magnetic activity. Over roughly 11 years, the Sun moves from a period of calm (solar minimum) to a peak of activity (solar maximum) and back again. At maximum, dark patches called sunspots pepper the visible face of the Sun. At minimum, days or weeks can pass with the disk entirely clear.

The 11-year length is an average; individual cycles have ranged from about 9 to 14 years. Astronomers count cycles from the minimum before the first numbered cycle in 1755. Solar Cycle 25 began in December 2019.

Sunspots and magnetic fields

Sunspots are regions where intense, tangled magnetic fields break through the photosphere and suppress the normal convective rise of hot gas. The surface there is cooler by roughly 1,500 °C relative to its surroundings — still thousands of degrees, but cool enough to look dark by contrast.

As the cycle progresses, the Sun's global magnetic field gradually becomes more complex. Sunspot pairs emerge with opposite magnetic polarities, and the tilt and position of these pairs follow predictable rules first described by George Ellery Hale in the early twentieth century. By solar maximum, the polar field has reversed, setting the clock for the next cycle.

Solar maximum and its effects

Near maximum, the Sun produces more ultraviolet and X-ray radiation, and it launches more coronal mass ejections — billions of tonnes of magnetised plasma hurled into space. When one of these hits Earth's magnetic field, it can trigger a geomagnetic storm. Strong storms light up the aurora well outside the polar regions and, in extreme cases, can disrupt power grids and satellite operations.

The largest geomagnetic storm on record, the Carrington Event of 1859, occurred near a solar maximum. Telegraph operators reported sparks from their equipment and fires in some offices. A comparable event today could cause widespread disruption to electrical infrastructure.

Solar flares — sudden bursts of electromagnetic radiation — arrive at Earth in about eight minutes (travelling at the speed of light) and can degrade high-frequency radio communications used by aviation and emergency services.

Predicting the next peak

Solar Cycle 25, which began in December 2019, has been more active than many forecasters initially expected. The NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center and an international panel update their peak forecasts periodically; as of the mid-2020s, Cycle 25 was tracking above the predicted average. Forecasting remains imprecise: the Sun's interior is hidden, and the processes that set cycle strength are not yet fully understood.

Some cycles are notably weak. The Maunder Minimum (roughly 1645–1715) saw very few sunspots and coincided with the coldest part of the Little Ice Age in Europe, though the causal link is debated. Long-term solar behaviour appears to correlate modestly with climate, but greenhouse gas concentrations are now the dominant driver of observed warming.
