Solar System

Gas giants versus ice giants

Our Solar System's four giant planets split into two distinct families. Jupiter and Saturn — the gas giants — are dominated by hydrogen and helium. Uranus and Neptune — the ice giants — hold a very different mix of materials. Understanding the difference matters not just for our own solar system but for the thousands of giant planets found around other stars.

Two families of giant planets

All four giants formed in the outer Solar System, beyond the "snow line" — the distance from the Sun at which water and other compounds freeze into solid ice grains. That icy material was the raw material for their solid cores, which then pulled in gas from the surrounding nebula. But the four planets did not grow identically, and the result is two distinct categories that differ in composition, internal structure, and behaviour.

What gas giants are made of

Jupiter and Saturn are overwhelmingly hydrogen and helium, the same elements that dominate the Sun. Jupiter holds about 318 times Earth's mass; Saturn, despite being almost as large, holds only about 95 Earth masses — it is so deficient in heavy elements relative to its size that it is the least dense planet in the Solar System. A body of water large enough to hold it, Saturn would float.

Beneath their cloud tops, hydrogen in both planets transitions under enormous pressure into a metallic state — one that conducts electricity — which drives their powerful magnetic fields. Jupiter's interior likely has a diffuse, poorly defined core enriched in heavier elements, but this is still being studied; the Juno spacecraft has been measuring Jupiter's gravitational field to probe its interior.

Ice giants: a different interior

Uranus and Neptune are sometimes called ice giants, but the name can mislead. The "ices" in their interiors — water, ammonia, and methane — are not frozen solid. At the pressures inside these planets, these substances exist as hot, dense, ionic fluids rather than ordinary ice. They give Uranus and Neptune a fundamentally different internal composition from Jupiter and Saturn: heavier elements make up a much larger fraction of their total mass.

Methane in their upper atmospheres absorbs red light, giving both planets their blue-green colour. Neptune, the more active of the two, has the fastest measured winds in the Solar System, exceeding 2,000 km/h. Uranus is unusual in being tilted about 98° on its axis — nearly on its side — so its poles point almost directly at the Sun for decades at a time.

Why Uranus emits almost no internal heat while Neptune radiates more energy than it receives from the Sun is not well understood. The two worlds are otherwise similar in size and composition, and this discrepancy is an active area of research.

Giants in other solar systems

Exoplanet surveys have found that giant planets of all kinds are common. "Hot Jupiters" — gas giants orbiting closer to their stars than Mercury orbits the Sun — were among the first exoplanets discovered and were a surprise, since our own gas giants form far from the Sun. "Sub-Neptunes" and "mini-Neptunes" — planets smaller than Neptune but larger than Earth — turn out to be among the most common planet types found so far, yet our own Solar System has none. Understanding gas and ice giant formation helps explain why planetary systems look so different from our own.
